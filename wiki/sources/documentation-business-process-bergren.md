# Documentation: How to Write a Clear Business Process Anyone Can Follow

**Source:** Jen Bergren's blog (workshop and course content distillation), 2023-08-14
**Scope:** Practical guide for writing business process documentation; aimed at non-technical teams (ops, agency, RevOps context)

---

## Core Framework: Three Phases

Bergren's model has three sequential phases, with "writing steps" first to overcome blank-page fear before worrying about structure or visuals.

### Phase 1: Writing the Steps

**Living documentation mindset** — first draft is never perfect. Don't wait for the right tool; use whatever lets you start.

**Overcoming blank-page fear:**
- Talk through the process, transcribe with otter.ai / Zoom transcription
- For software processes: screen-recording tools (Guidde, Iorad, Tango, Scribe) record mouse flow and can transcribe narration

**Recipe metaphor** — write process steps like a cooking recipe: what do you do first, second, third? This helps surface implicit steps that experienced performers do automatically.

**Tips for clear steps:**
- Write for someone who has never done this before (can an 8-year-old follow it?)
- Use second-person imperative: "Do this, do that" — not "I do this"
- Use titles, not names (people change roles)
- No jargon or abbreviations
- Include what triggers the process to START and END

**User test with an unfamiliar reviewer** — not the person who does the task. Ask: can they follow it to completion without asking questions?

**Conditional if/then steps** — when a process branches, use:
- Anchor links (TOC-style) to jump to Branch A or Branch B within the same doc
- Or link out to a separate article for each branch
- Consider a process map/flowchart to show the fork visually

### Phase 2: Naming & Structure

**Naming conventions:**
- Use "How to [verb]..." or "What is [topic]?" format — mirrors how people search
- Include department prefix if needed: "Marketing: How to create a content calendar"
- Draft 5–10 name candidates; include words people would actually search
- The question format forces the writer to articulate what they're teaching

**Recommended template sections:**
1. Last Updated (and Who Updated)
2. Owner — who approves changes / who to ask questions
3. "You might be looking for..." — links to commonly confused docs
4. History / Context (skippable via TOC on repeat visits)
5. **Process steps** (the main section)
6. "Before / After" links — what precedes or follows this process
7. Related Articles / Resources

**Why a template matters:** users learn where to look across all docs (encourages adoption); writers have a scaffold (removes creation barrier); top section gives freshness signals (when updated, who updated).

**Table of contents** — link from TOC to section anchors; lets users jump to exactly the step they need rather than re-reading the whole doc.

**Formatting tips:**
- Bolding + heading hierarchy to visually separate info
- Bullet points, short sentences, simple language
- Callout boxes or colored tables for important highlights
- "In progress" / "coming soon" markers with brackets `[TBD]` are OK — signals living doc; makes others less afraid to edit

### Phase 3: Visuals

Add visuals AFTER writing steps, not during — internal task-switching between words and images is cognitively expensive.

**Visual types:** flowcharts, screenshots, GIFs, videos, embedded slideshows, infographics, emojis, charts

**Why text-first:** easier to edit; no special software; faster to translate; audio/video are hard to skim.

**When to add audio/video first:** if talking is easier for you, record then transcribe. Just make sure written steps also exist for skimmability.

---

## Key Insight

> Documentation is content. Knowledge sharing. Eliminating knowledge gaps and silos. Storing info where people can find it — that's knowledge distribution.

The real goal is not "write documentation" — it's building a culture where knowledge leaves heads and enters shared systems. The three-phase framework is a scaffold for exactly that.
